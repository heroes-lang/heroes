The author

· moonage daydream ·

Giuseppe Arici has been writing software for over twenty years: Java, C#, Rails, iOS, Go, microservices, DevOps. A cap for every language. He is a co-founder and CTO at Codermine, in Brescia, and the author of Heroes of code, a book on the history of programming languages, published on Amazon. He had never written a compiler before this one.

In his own words

I caught the tail end of COBOL, started out in Java, passed through C# and the Microsoft world, until Rails made me jump to Unix. From there Linux, the Mac, the iPhone: for years mobile was my line of work. Then Go came along, the Gopher won me over, and with it microservices, backends and every kind of architecture. These days I also handle DevOps. I code less than I would like and manage more than I will admit, AI agents included.

I have consulted for companies of every stripe and walked away with one conviction: the real problems are almost always organizational, not technological. So I do not tie myself to one technology, even though I have my favorites. A polyglot programmer, with all due modesty: a cap for every language, a t-shirt for every conference. My heroes are John Backus, Dennis Ritchie and Bob Kahn. I am passionate about history, and about the history of computing in particular. I co-founded #pragma mark, the Italian association that hosts conferences on the Apple ecosystem, and have spoken at Pragma Conference and Codemotion.

The book · lady stardust ·

Heroes of code, subtitled A journey through the years that shook programming languages. In Italian, Gli eroi del codice.

It opens at midnight, on an empty file, with a programmer looking for the words to tell the story of programming languages. Then a voice behind him: David Bowie. What follows is a dream journey through the rooms where it all started, Bletchley Park, MIT, Zurich, Bell Labs, Oslo, Silicon Valley, and the people who invented the way we talk to machines. At dawn the dream ends, the guide dissolves, and only the road is left.

The book came first, and in that order. A language named after a Bowie album, with song titles for section headings, is not a joke somebody had halfway through: it is the same work seen from the other side, and the quotation marks are the album’s own, put there in 1977 to undercut the word. It also explains a seat on the review panel. Somebody who has just spent twenty-one chapters on FORTRAN, ALGOL, Lisp and Simula is badly placed to believe a design question is new, so the panel keeps a historian: the one seat with no veto and the strictest rule on evidence. Who wrote this

The compiler is written by an AI assistant, working under a contract kept in the repository and read at the start of every session. That contract is not a prompt: it is a set of rules with dates and reasons, most of them written the day something went wrong, and it is the reason this page can be specific about who did what.

The author sets the direction, ratifies every design decision, and reads the record. Some outcomes came from a question rather than an instruction. When the cost of a safety check was being haggled over as one number, the author asked a different question: is the cost paid when the program is compiled, or while it runs? Answering it meant asking where a foreign thread enters rather than where the damage shows, and that moved the check from three wrong places to the right one.

It runs the other way too. A design sitting was ordered by the author over the assistant’s twice-stated advice to leave the question alone. Five judges refused it, on three vetoes and none of them about cost, and the mechanism the author had asked for turned out to exist already. The author ratified the refusal in one line.

The rule behind that is short: name the author where a question, a correction or a refusal produced the finding, name the panel seat where a seat found it, and name nobody where the work was ordinary.
