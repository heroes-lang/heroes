Thanks

· sons of the silent age ·

Nothing in this language is new. Every piece of it was worked out by somebody else first, usually decades ago, often in a paper that also says what it cost them. These are their names.

Where a language was designed by a team, the designers are named; where it was one person, that is said too. Nobody here was asked, and nobody here is responsible for what was done with their idea.

The substrate, and the tools

Heroes has no standard library by design, so everything a program touches arrives from C. The compiler emits C11 and hands it to clang, which type-checks every foreign declaration against the real header. The bootstrap compiler that got the project to self-hosting was written in Rust.

  • C: Dennis Ritchie, who designed it; Ken Thompson, who wrote B, the language C came out of, and Unix with Ritchie; and Brian Kernighan, who co-wrote the book that taught it to everyone. The whole of Part 11 of the design rests on the fact that their language is still there, and still linkable.
  • LLVM, clang: Chris Lattner. Heroes does not verify a foreign declaration itself; it arranges for clang to do it. A compiler that outsources its hardest checking to another compiler owes that compiler a mention.
  • Rust: the Rust team, and Graydon Hoare, who started it. The bootstrap compiler is Rust written in a deliberately narrow subset, and that subset is why porting it to Heroes was mechanical rather than a rewrite.

The value model, which is the whole design

One decision makes everything else small: values are never aliased, so reference cycles cannot be built, so reference counting is complete with no garbage collector. That is not this project’s discovery. It is the current research frontier, and it has a published argument.

  • Hylo, formerly Val: Dimitri Racordon, Denys Shabalin, Daniel Zheng, Dave Abrahams and Brennan Saeta, the authors of Native Implementation of Mutable Value Semantics. The sentence Heroes depends on is theirs: references cannot be stored in variables or fields, hence all values form disjoint trees. Everything the language does not need (a borrow checker, a cycle collector, lifetime annotations) is absent because of that one line.
  • Swift: Chris Lattner, Doug Gregor, John McCall, Ted Kremenek and Joe Groff. Copy-on-write over reference counting, inout parameters (this language’s @), and leading-dot variants. Swift is also the reassuring precedent, because it is not a research language: it has shipped this model for over a decade. Heroes takes the half that works and declines class, and with it weak and unowned.
  • APL, J, K: Kenneth E. Iverson, Roger Hui and Arthur Whitney. Sixty years of value semantics over whole values, with no references in the surface language, and the evidence that this model is not what makes a language slow.
  • R: Ross Ihaka and Robert Gentleman. MATLAB: Cleve Moler. Copy-on-write value semantics in the two languages that carried statistics and numerical computing for decades: millions of people who never needed a mutable reference to do real work.

Safety without a garbage collector

A Heroes program must not segfault and must not corrupt memory, and the ways to get there without a collector were mapped out by other people first.

  • Cyclone: Dan Grossman, Michael Hicks, Trevor Jim and Greg Morrisett. References only as function parameters, which deletes lifetime annotations from the language. This project uses their rule twice: it is the discipline the Rust bootstrap compiler was written in, and it is why the port to Heroes was mechanical. The rule has their name on it in the repository.
  • Modula-3: Luca Cardelli, James Donahue, Lucille Glassman, Mick Jordan, Bill Kalsow and Greg Nelson. Unsafety with a fence around it: the model for keeping every dangerous operation inside one named, auditable place instead of spread through the language.

How small a language is allowed to be

The specification has a hard ceiling measured in tokens, and the self-hosting compiler has a size it is not allowed to exceed. Both numbers were calibrated against work from the 1970s and 1980s rather than invented.

  • Pascal, Modula, Oberon: Niklaus Wirth, with Jürg Gutknecht on Oberon. record and variant come from here. So do both calibrations: the Oberon report at sixteen pages is how long a specification is allowed to be, and Pascal-P4 at roughly four thousand lines is how big a self-hosting compiler is allowed to be. Wirth also wrote an entire operating system in a language with no raw pointers, and nothing argues harder for this whole approach.

The types, and one idea deliberately declined

  • ML: Robin Milner and the group at Edinburgh, among them Michael J. C. Gordon. The product-and-sum pair Heroes calls record and variant, and the tradition tagged unions come out of.
  • The arrow: Alonzo Church. -> in a function type is the single most familiar piece of syntax in existence, and it reached Heroes through ML, Haskell, Rust, Swift and Python’s type hints without changing shape. It was kept exactly as it was found.
  • The subroutine: David Wheeler, on EDSAC in 1951. It is worth saying out loud that somebody had to invent the idea of calling a piece of code from more than one place.
  • Type inference: J. Roger Hindley and Robin Milner. This one is a thank-you for something Heroes does not use: whole-program inference is beautiful and it moves errors far away from their cause, which this language cannot afford. Knowing precisely what was being given up, and why, is what their work makes possible.

What the language feels like to use

The habits, the small refusals, and the shape of the command line. Almost none of this is original either, and that is the point: a reader who knows one of these languages already knows most of Heroes.

  • Go: Robert Griesemer, Rob Pike and Ken Thompson. One canonical format with no options, unused variables as errors, a line that ends without a semicolon, and the nerve to ship for a decade without generics.
  • Nim: Andreas Rumpf. The foreign-function mechanism, the per-module build cache, and nim r, which is heroes run. Also the standing warning the project repeats to itself: copy Nim’s surface, never its implementation.
  • Zig: Andrew Kelley. Tests living in the file they test, and NUL-terminated strings that can cross into C without a conversion.
  • ABC: Leo Geurts, Lambert Meertens and Steven Pemberton, at CWI in Amsterdam. Indentation as the syntax, with no braces at all. Guido van Rossum worked on ABC for years before carrying that into Python, and Peter Landin had named the rule in 1966. Heroes takes it for two reasons at once: a brace nobody writes is a token nobody spends, and the models that write code have read so much Python that this is one piece of layout they already know.
  • Python: Guido van Rossum, and Tim Peters for import this, which became heroes this, carried inside the binary rather than in a document that drifts. It is here.
  • Odin, Jai: the author of Odin, who publishes as gingerBill, and Jonathan Blow. One uniform way to write a declaration, whatever is being declared.
  • Ruby: Yukihiro Matsumoto. The reading rhythm (named blocks, named arguments, dot chains), taken as a target for how code should scan out loud, with the metaprogramming underneath left firmly alone.

Errors worth reading, and typed holes

  • Rust: the Rust team, and Elm: Evan Czaplicki. Between them they proved that the quality of an error message changes the whole experience of a language, and that it is a design deliverable rather than plumbing. Heroes aims the same effort at a different reader: a diagnostic here has to be fixable without opening a second file.
  • Idris: Edwin Brady. Agda: Ulf Norell, and Catarina Coquand on the first version. Typed holes, moved out of proof assistants and into ordinary code: in Heroes, ??? is a legal expression, and the compiler answers it by saying what belongs in the gap.

Immutability at scale

  • Erlang: Joe Armstrong, Robert Virding and Mike Williams. Immutability enforced absolutely, in systems with famously long uptimes, which is the proof that this is not a toy constraint. The motive is inverted and the inversion is the instructive part: Erlang gives up sharing to get isolation between processes, Heroes gives it up to make a single function readable on its own.

Compiling to C, an old unglamorous trick

Heroes emits C and lets a C compiler do the machine-specific work. That was the dominant technique for languages built by very few people, and it is still the reason one person can finish this.

  • cfront: Bjarne Stroustrup. C++ began as a program that emitted C.
  • Vala: Jürg Billeter, Raffaele Sandrini and Rico Tzschichholz. Chicken Scheme: Felix Winkelmann. Cython: Robert Bradshaw and Stefan Behnel. Three working languages that went out through C, each one evidence the route is a choice and not a compromise.
  • Early Haskell: designed by a committee, among them Paul Hudak, John Hughes, Simon Peyton Jones and Philip Wadler. It reached real machines through C as well, in a language nobody would call low-level.

Cited as a warning, which is also a gift

Some of these languages are here because of what they got wrong, or what they paid for. A precedent that says do not do this, here is the bill is worth as much as one that says do this. It is also much rarer, because producing one usually costs somebody years.

  • Lua, with Nelua and Pallene: Roberto Ierusalimschy, Luiz Henrique de Figueiredo and Waldemar Celes. The precedent for a small language that compiles, and the lesson that one universal boxed table makes every call into C require marshalling. Heroes gave up the universal table for that reason.
  • APL again, in the other direction: ultra-compact notation is the one place where this project cites Iverson against itself: brevity that a human eye can learn is exactly what a language model fails at.
  • R and MATLAB again: both document, honestly, the same failure mode Heroes has: write a loop that changes one element at a time over a shared structure and you pay for a copy every time. The design says so out loud instead of hiding it.
  • Unison: Paul Chiusano and Rúnar Bjarnason. Code stored as a tree with names as labels: the origin of an idea that was explored here at length and then dropped in favour of one readable syntax.
  • Ada: Jean Ichbiah for Ada 83, and Tucker Taft for Ada 95 through 2012. One refusal was borrowed whole: passing the same variable twice to one call when both can be written to. Ada allowed it for thirty-three years, watched what happened, and made it illegal in 2012. Heroes made it illegal on day one, on their evidence, and the compiler offers the same repair.

And the name

David Bowie, for “Heroes” (1977). The quotation marks are his own, and they stayed. The name, the section titles on this site, and the book the language grew out of all come from there. The rule the project holds itself to is that the personality stays in the packaging: song titles as nods, never a lyric, and never a word of it inside an error message or a library name.
