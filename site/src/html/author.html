Giuseppe Arici has been writing software for over twenty years: Java, C#, Rails, iOS, Go, microservices, DevOps. A cap for every language. He is a co-founder and CTO at Codermine, in Brescia, and the author of Heroes of code, a book on the history of programming languages, published on Amazon. He had never written a compiler before this one.

In his own words

I have been writing software for over twenty years. I caught the tail end of COBOL, started out in Java, passed through C# and the Microsoft world, until Rails made me jump to Unix. From there Linux, the Mac, the iPhone: for years mobile was my line of work. Then Go came along, the Gopher won me over, and with it microservices, backends and every kind of architecture. These days I also handle DevOps. I code less than I would like and manage more than I will admit, AI agents included.

I have done consulting for companies of every stripe and walked away with one conviction: the real problems are almost always organizational, not technological. Which is why I do not tie myself to a single technology, even though I have my favorites. I call myself, with all due modesty, a polyglot programmer: a cap for every language, a t-shirt for every conference. My heroes are John Backus, Dennis Ritchie and Bob Kahn.

I am passionate about history in general and the history of computing in particular. I co-founded #pragma mark, the Italian association that hosts conferences on the Apple ecosystem, and have spoken at events like Pragma Conference and Codemotion.

The book · lady stardust ·

Heroes of code, subtitled A journey through the years that shook programming languages. In Italian, Gli eroi del codice.

It opens at midnight, on an empty file, with a programmer looking for the words to tell the story of programming languages. Then a voice behind him: David Bowie. What follows is a dream journey through the rooms where it all started, Bletchley Park, MIT, Zurich, Bell Labs, Oslo, Silicon Valley, and the people who invented the way we talk to machines. At dawn the dream ends, the guide dissolves, and only the road is left.

Twenty-one chapters, and they are titled like a record rather than like a reference:

Underneath those titles: FORTRAN, COBOL, ALGOL, Dijkstra, Wirth, Unix and C, Lisp, type systems, Simula, Smalltalk, Prolog, SQL, the multiparadigm era, exception handling, purity and monads, and Erlang's supervision trees. Plus notes, references and a timeline, because a story about history that cannot be checked is a story about something else.

What the book has to do with the language

The book came first, and in that order. A language named after a Bowie album, with song titles for section headings, is not a joke somebody had halfway through: it is the same work seen from the other side, and the quotation marks are the album's own, put there in 1977 to undercut the word.

It also explains a seat on the review panel. Somebody who has just spent twenty-one chapters on FORTRAN, ALGOL, Lisp and Simula is badly placed to believe a design question is new, so the panel keeps a historian: the one seat with no veto and the strictest rule on evidence, cite the source or the precedent is inadmissible.
