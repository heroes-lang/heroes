The panel

· under pressure ·

Nothing changes in this language because it seemed like a good idea. Five judges review it first, four of them can stop it, and each one is shown different evidence on purpose.

A language built by one person has one failure mode above all others: the author agrees with themselves. There is nobody in the room whose job is to say that a construct is elegant and useless, that a rule reads well and cannot be implemented, or that the thing being invented already has a name and a paper. So the project built that room.

The panel is convened before the language changes: the specification, the design document's own rules, surface syntax or meaning, a whole class of diagnostic, or the architecture underneath (the backend, the intermediate representation, the shape of the command line). Changing how the project teaches, or how it writes things down, needs no panel. The line is drawn at the thing a program written in Heroes can see.

Five seats, four vetoes · five years ·

Each seat defends exactly one thing, and each is handed a different brief. That is the mechanism, not an accident of convenience: a judge who has read the design document already knows what the answer is supposed to be, so the seat that speaks for the reader is never allowed to see it.

  • The readerveto

    Speaks for the objective: can a language model understand this from the page in front of it? Receives only the specification and sample programs, never the design document and never the repository, so its verdict is an experiment rather than an opinion. Its veto covers anything whose meaning cannot be worked out locally.

  • The compiler engineerveto

    Speaks for the ceiling: what will this cost to implement, and is it core or sugar? Its brief must contain the actual compiler source, not a description of it. Its veto covers a new construct in the core and anything that pushes the compiler past the size one person can finish.

  • The C pragmatistveto

    Speaks for the founding constraint: there is no standard library, so everything a program touches arrives from C. Its brief must include the C that a real binding would need under the proposal, and the seat compiles it or shows why it cannot. Its veto covers anything that breaks the C boundary.

  • The spec wardenveto

    Speaks for the budget: the whole specification has to keep fitting in a prompt, so every addition has to name what it removes. Its brief must carry the real token count before and after, measured, never estimated. Its veto fires when the measured budget goes over the ceiling.

  • The historianadvisory

    Speaks for everyone who solved this before. It is the only seat with no veto, and the only one under a search obligation: dates, line counts and claims have to be verified against sources, because an invented precedent is worse than none. It is the seat below.

Two rules hold the whole thing together. Every judge has to state what would prove them wrong, so a verdict is a claim that can fail rather than a preference. And the panel never blocks the work: the synthesis adopts the most conservative reading, marks it provisional, and queues it for the author to ratify, so a review can be slow without the project stopping.

Nothing lands on a verdict alone. A design change needs the sitting written up in the repository, one line in the decision log saying what was decided and why, and its own commit citing the verdict. That record is what separates reasoning from improvisation once the reasons are three months old. There have been 83 sittings, counted on 2026-08-18, and they are all in the repository, including the ones where the proposal lost.

The historian, and why the past holds a seat · look back in anger ·

Almost every question a new language runs into has been answered by somebody else, usually decades ago, often in a paper that says exactly what it cost. Deciding it from scratch is not brave, it is expensive: the design gets a worse answer and the project pays for the same lesson twice.

So one seat does nothing but look backwards. It has no veto, because precedent is evidence rather than authority, and it carries the strictest rule on the panel: every date, every line count, every claim about what another language did has to be checked against a source and cited. A precedent nobody verified is the most confident kind of wrong answer, and this is the one seat where that failure is most likely.

It matters more here than it would on another project, because this language did not begin as a language. Its author wrote Heroes of code, a history of programming languages framed as a dream journey with Bowie as the guide, out on 20 September 2026: twenty-one chapters on FORTRAN, COBOL, ALGOL, Lisp, Simula, Smalltalk, Prolog and the rest, with notes, references and a timeline behind them. The history here is not decoration bolted onto a design, it is where the design came from, and where the name came from. What it borrowed, and from whom

Nothing here is invented from nothing. Each borrowing is written down so the departures are deliberate, and so that a reader who knows one of these languages already knows most of Heroes:

  • Hylo and Val supply the load-bearing idea: mutable value semantics, memory safety with no borrow checker. If values are never aliased, reference cycles cannot be built, so reference counting is complete with no collector. That is the reason the whole design stays small.
  • Swift is the reassurance, because it is not a research language: its structs, arrays and strings are values with copy-on-write over reference counting, mechanically what Heroes does. Its inout is this language's @, and its leading-dot variant syntax is here too. Heroes declines class, and with it the cycles, weak and unowned.
  • Cyclone gives the discipline the compiler itself is written in: references only as function parameters, which removes lifetime annotations entirely, and which is why porting that compiler into Heroes was mechanical rather than a rewrite.
  • Go gives canonical formatting, unused variables as errors, documentation by adjacency, and the rule that decides where a line ends without semicolons. Also the patience: it shipped for a decade without generics.
  • Nim gives the FFI mechanism, the per-module build cache, and heroes run itself. It is also the cautionary tale, so the standing rule is to copy Nim's surface and never its implementation.
  • Zig gives tests written in the source file they test, and the NUL-terminated string design that lets a Heroes string cross into C.
  • Ada gives one refusal: the same variable cannot be passed twice to one call when both parameters can write to it. Ada lived with that for thirty-three years and made it illegal in 2012, for the reason this language was built around.
  • Pascal, ML and Wirth give record and variant as the product and sum pair, the sixteen-page Oberon report as the calibration for how long a specification may be, and Pascal-P4 as the size a self-hosting compiler is allowed to reach.
  • Idris and Agda give typed holes, moved from proof assistants to code generation: ??? is a question the compiler answers.
  • Rust and Elm give the demonstration that error message quality changes everything, aimed here at a different reader.
  • Erlang gives the proof that immutability at scale works, with the motive inverted: Erlang removes sharing for concurrency, Heroes removes it for local readability.
  • APL, K, R and MATLAB are cited in two directions at once. Against, on notation: ultra-compact syntax fails for models. In favour, on the value model: sixty years of value semantics over whole arrays, and the evidence that this model is not what makes a language slow. They also document its failure mode honestly, which is Heroes' too.
  • cfront, Vala, Chicken Scheme and early Haskell are the C-emission lineage: historically the dominant technique for languages built by very few people, and the reason clang gets to check every foreign declaration.
  • Python gives one thing, and it is a joke with a rule inside it: import this became heroes this, twenty lines the compiler carries in its own binary rather than in a document that could drift. They are here.

The borrowings that were refused are recorded the same way. Ruby's reading rhythm is worth taking and its mechanism is the worst case for locality. APL's value model is in and its notation is out. A refusal on this project has to name the program or the compiler fact that would prove it wrong, exactly like a feature does, because a permanent no is otherwise the one design act nobody has to justify.

Who is building this · absolute beginners ·

One person: Giuseppe Arici, partner and CTO at Codermine, in Brescia, with over twenty years of software behind him, in Java, C#, Rails, iOS, Go, microservices and DevOps, and a book on the history of programming languages to his name. He had never written a compiler before this one.

That is the premise of the project rather than an apology for it, and it is why everything here is written down as it happens: the journal, the decision log, the sittings above, the failed predictions and the measurements. A first compiler, built by somebody who reads the history of this craft for a living, in the open. Who he is, and the book →

The name is Bowie's, and so is the irony: he put the quotation marks around “Heroes” in 1977 to undercut the word. They stay in the packaging, and they never reach the language: you will never meet Bowie in an error message.

Everything the project decided, and everything it got wrong on the way, is public in the repository: the sittings above, the failed predictions, the defects with their symptoms, and the measurements that did not flatter the design.
