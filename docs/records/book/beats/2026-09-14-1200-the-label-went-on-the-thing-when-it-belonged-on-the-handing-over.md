2026-09-14 | M-cleanup-verdict | The question was whether the workshop should learn to tidy up after you. When you borrow something from outside — a door held open, a drawer pulled out — somebody has to put it back, and at the moment that somebody is always you, on every route out of the room, including the routes you take in a hurry because something went wrong. Other workshops have a standard answer for this. Before arguing about it, the rule here is that you go and count. The count was supposed to take an afternoon and settle which way to lean. Instead it broke its own question twice and then found a fault that had nothing to do with tidying up at all. The first break: the reason this work had been scheduled was that two of the existing arrangements were said to leave you holding an obligation on every route out. Neither does. One of them the workshop already discharges for you, on the spot, at the moment of borrowing — you were never holding anything. The other does leave you holding it, but if you walk out still holding it the whole building shouts at you and tells you how many you forgot. So of three things that might be left behind, exactly one is left behind quietly, and only that one was ever the subject. The second break was more interesting. It turned out the workshop **already** tidies up after you, thoroughly, on every route out including the panicked ones — but only for things it made itself. The things it borrows from outside are not on the list, and not as a matter of policy: they are not on the list because there is nothing written down to do with them. So the question shrank from *should this place learn to tidy up* to *can a borrowed thing be given a note saying how to put it back*. Five judges and a critic sat on that, and the answer was no, for a reason nobody in the room had walked in with. You cannot write the note on the **thing**. Two doors look identical and the same shape of key opens both, but one of them was given to you and the other you were only shown. Write *lock this when you leave* on the key and you will lock a door somebody else is standing behind. Somebody compiled exactly that: a program that reads three rows, given the note, reads nought rows and reports success. That is worse than the leak it was meant to fix, because a leak can be counted and a wrong answer that says it went fine cannot. The rule that decides this was already written down, years of small decisions old: **ask about the thing in your hand, never about the world around it**. Where the note belongs is on the handing over, not on the thing handed. Three judges reached that the expensive way, each from a different direction. Then the critic went through the count itself, which five judges had been handed as fact, and found four numbers wrong — including the one that mattered most. The programs said to be leaking all stop dead immediately afterwards. Not one of them leaks and then carries on. So the fault is real and the hurry was not, and the correction is written underneath the original rather than over it. And in the middle of all this, the count found something else. A probe asked the database how many things it still had open and was told nought, while in the same breath the database refused to close on the grounds that something was still open. Two sentences that cannot both be true. The same programme written in the other language said one, and one was right. Asking whether two borrowed things were the same thing had been answering **yes, always** — which meant the commonest line anybody writes when borrowing, the one that checks whether you got anything at all, had not worked since the day the feature landed. It had been landed the day before. And the single test that would have caught it had been written the same day, and passed, because it happened to ask the one question where the right answer and the broken answer are the same word.
