2026-09-08 | M-closures-verdict | A note had been sitting in the design document since the first week, in the confident tone of somebody who has just seen how a thing will go: closures are easy here, they cost about a hundred and fifty lines and about sixty words of the specification, and they will delete that handful of little one-line helpers that only exist to be handed around. Five weeks later the day came to do it, and the first thing anybody did was count the helpers. There are twenty-one. Every single one of them lives in an example program, and four of the seven files holding them are the programs whose whole subject is handing functions around — they are the demonstration, not the injury. The compiler itself, fifty-five thousand lines of it, has none. It hands a function to something else in four places in the entire body of working code, and not one of those four wants to carry anything with it. Then the cost was measured instead of remembered, by making the compiler count its own wounds rather than reading them off: two hundred and eighty-two places where a list of possibilities would have to grow, spread through seventy-four of a hundred and ninety files, five of them already at a length somebody had decided was the limit, and the new piece of the parser with nowhere legal to live, because the language forbids the very circle it would need. The specification clause came in at twice the estimate. And then the part that decided it, which nobody could have argued to: the machine underneath declares that a function is eight bytes, and a function that carries two small things with it is thirty-two. Handed to a real C library through a real cast, the compiler that checks everything said nothing at all, with every warning it owns turned on, and the program died. Written into an array, it walked off the end of it and lost three quarters of what it was carrying without a word. Asked whether two of them were equal, it read the first eight bytes and answered about the wrong half. So the thing was refused, and the refusal was written down to the same standard a feature is held to: here is what would have to exist for us to be wrong, and here is the shape of the machinery it would need. The pleasing part is who found what. One judge reads nothing but the specification and never sees the code; another compiles C against real headers and never argues. The first said the question was mis-aimed, because what actually stops a reader is not the missing feature but four missing sentences about functions the language already has. The second proved the missing feature would corrupt memory. Neither could have said the other's thing. And a third, sent to look for a language that had refused this on purpose and expected to come back empty-handed, came back with four, one of which has been refusing it since nineteen eighty-eight and writes its own compiler in itself. The specification grew by thirty words that day and not one of them is a type. It says that anonymous functions do not exist — because the refusal had been made in a document readers never see — and it says which half of a list a filter keeps, which turns out to be the one thing a signature can never tell you, since keeping and discarding have exactly the same shape.
