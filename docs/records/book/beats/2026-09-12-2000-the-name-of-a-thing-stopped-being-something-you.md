2026-09-12 | M-reflection-verdict | The question was whether a program should be able to ask a thing what it is made of. The council said no, three times, and each time the reason written down for saying no turned out to be false, so the reasons were replaced with ones that can be measured. Then came the part nobody had planned. A program that sends data out has to write the names of its own fields by hand, as text, and one wrong letter goes out into the world with nothing complaining. The brief that went to the judges asked the wrong question about it, and the judges answered the right one anyway: four of the five invented something that was not on the list, and they invented it in two pairs, each pair arriving at the same idea without having seen the other. One of the two was a single small mark. Write the name of a field the way you write the name of anything else, and the compiler knows whether it exists. The one who could only read the language's written description and the one who could read the whole machine proposed it in the same hour, in two different spellings, and the author chose the spelling that the machine had not already built, for a reason about reading rather than about work: the other mark was already doing two jobs, and a third would have made a line impossible to understand without knowing what was written somewhere else. The price was sixty-eight places in the compiler that stopped compiling until each one said what it wanted, which is the language charging its own author the way it charges everybody. And the proof was one small program written twice: with the names as text, four deliberate typos and not one caught; with the names as names, five and all five.
