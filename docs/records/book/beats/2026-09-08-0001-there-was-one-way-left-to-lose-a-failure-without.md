2026-09-08 | M-discard-refusal | There was one way left to lose a failure without anybody noticing, and it was the polite one: a line that said, in the language's own words, *I know this produces something and I am throwing it away on purpose*. Thrown away with the value went the news that it had failed, and the program walked on and finished with the number that means all is well. So that line became an error, and then the interesting part started, which is that a wall is only as good as the space beside it. The first wall built had a door in it five characters wide, and the second, wider one had something worse: the way around it also worked on the thing the wall was there to protect, so anybody taught to climb over would have learnt the trick that defeats the whole idea. In the end the honest thing was to build the wall where a wall works and, where it does not, write down plainly what gets through — because a reader who is promised a guarantee and given most of one is worse off than a reader who is told the shape of the hole. Then the small comedy: the way the language now asks you to say *yes, I really am dropping this* made the machine underneath complain about a variable nobody reads, which was fixed not by deleting the variable but by telling the machine the silence was deliberate — the same sentence, in a different language, three weeks apart. And the way anybody knows any of this worked is not an argument. Two hundred and thirty-six deliberate mistakes were planted in real programs, of the exact kind this was about, and every single one of them refused to compile.
