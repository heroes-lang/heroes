2026-09-04 | M-corpus-depth | The collection of example programs had grown to cover every feature of the language, and it still had a hole nobody had named: every program in it was small. Nine more were written to be big, or slow, or checked against answers somebody else had published decades ago. Each one reported back on the compiler, which is what the collection is for. But the one that changed the most was a meeting called to answer a narrow question about how the compiler lays out memory. Two reviewers refused the question, and one of them, while refusing it, measured where the memory was actually going: more than half of every function's working space was being spent on bookkeeping the compiler created and then never read. Removing it halved the space every program needs, doubled how deeply a program can call itself, and — days after somebody had written it down as a defect — quietly fixed a failure on Windows that nobody had aimed at. The lesson the author wanted kept was smaller and sharper: the reviewers were asked one thing and answered a better one, and the only reason anybody noticed was that the refusal came with numbers.
