2026-09-05 | M-c-callbacks | Some C functions do not want a value from you, they want a piece of your program: sort this list, and here is how to compare two items; call me back when the program is about to end. The language had no way to hand one over, so every library that works that way was simply out of reach, and the answer had always been "keep a bit of C around for that". The fix turned out to be a permission rather than a machine. The compiler had been printing exactly the right thing for months without being allowed to say so: a function written in this language comes out the other side already shaped like the thing C wants, so the address goes across with nothing wrapped around it. Thirty-two lines. What took the day was everything standing next to those thirty-two lines. Handing your own code to a stranger is only safe in one direction, and the direction is the whole argument: an address going out points at something this compiler read and checked, while an address coming back points at something nobody here has ever seen — so a callback may be given and never received, and three different ways of receiving one were shut, including one nobody had thought of. Then a meeting was called to decide whether to teach the language a small new word so that the sorting function would work too. The meeting refused, and the reason is the part worth keeping: somebody stopped arguing and counted. In one real library's header there are a hundred and six of these hand-me-your-code functions, and the thing the meeting was convened about was the fourth most common reason they fail, at two and a half percent. The most common reason, at two thirds, was something else entirely and nobody had mentioned it. The word was not added; the hole was written down honestly instead, in the order the counting found rather than the order the meeting had assumed. And underneath all of it a smaller thing was quietly fixed. If you handed C an empty slot where a piece of your program should have been, the program died instantly with no words at all — and the trick to naming it is that a program calling nothing does not touch a forbidden address, it tries to run at one, which is a fingerprint nothing else leaves. Three machines, three different behaviours for the same mistake, one of them cheerfully carrying on as if nothing had happened. Which is the lesson: whether that mistake kills you is not a fact about your program or about C. It is a fact about the particular machine you are sitting at.
